### 1. Privacy: Aztec Tierra, Privacy Pools v2, Kohaku
**What happened**
- [Aztec: Aztec Tierra (Alpha V6) launches](https://aztec.network/blog/introducing-aztec-tierra), announced at 21:01 ([@aztecnetwork post](https://x.com/aztecnetwork/status/2117618265723912555)). According to the blog, it fixes known vulnerabilities from earlier versions (including a critical issue found in V5), and core components of the proof system are formally verified. Client-side proving on a local device takes about 1 second, twice as fast as the previous version. Message delivery from Ethereum to Aztec drops from about 2 minutes on average to as little as about 12 seconds. The network has more than 3,000 validators, with no privileged viewing keys and no centralized sequencer.
- [Ameen Soleimani: Privacy Pools v2 launches at Devcon](https://x.com/ameensol/status/2117545674233217157), 16:13. His visa was refused, so the team presented it.

- [Aztec: programmable privacy lets banks keep viewing keys in their own apps](https://x.com/aztecnetwork/status/2117641151486496772), 22:32; the network itself holds no keys.

**Why it matters**
- Privacy is moving from protocol research to products people can use directly. For developers, Aztec's private contract model differs from the EVM and is built with Noir.

### 2. Binance preliminary report: how 7702 authorizations slowed the response
**What happened**
- [Binance: preliminary investigation of the October 29 security incident](https://x.com/binance/status/2117626126214504729), published at 21:33. About $6.34 billion of assets were affected and about $2.85 billion has been frozen or intercepted. The attacker first took control of the internal wallet signing service and sent transactions outside the normal withdrawal flow. It then used the signing service to exploit EIP-7702: 50 signed transactions manipulated account nonces and set up a malicious delegation, giving an attacker-controlled contract partial execution rights over hot wallets.
- The report spells out why containment was hard: pausing withdrawals does not invalidate 7702 authorizations already onchain. The hot wallets use MPC, so there was no single key that could revoke the authorization at once, and the team had to write a dedicated procedure, which took longer. At 00:10 UTC on October 30, Binance sent a new authorization pointing to the zero address from the Binance14 hot wallet, clearing the delegated code.
- The intrusion path: in June an engineer downloaded a tampered Homebrew; in September another engineer downloaded a "take-home" repository with malicious code during a fake interview; on October 20 the attacker used an internal AI tool to take over more engineer accounts; on October 29 it used stolen credentials to deploy a container that triggered the attack. The FBI attributes it to North Korea.
- [dapplion interviews lightclient: the Binance theft used EIP-7702](https://x.com/dapplion/status/2117362449942708670), 04:05. lightclient, one of the 7702 authors, answers that "7702 is just an accelerant for an existing attack".
- [Eli Ben-Sasson: security depends on UX, and Starknet's native AA is good design](https://x.com/EliBenSasson/status/2117498362153075115), 13:05.

**Why it matters**
- The entry point was a compromised signing system, not a flaw in the 7702 protocol. But once a 7702 authorization is onchain, it exists independently of the exchange's risk controls, and revoking it requires the original account to sign another authorization. MPC and institutional wallets should put "how to revoke a 7702 delegation quickly" in their incident playbooks. At the protocol level, this is exactly what EIP-8476 (permanent opt-out) and the "authorizations with an expiry" proposals that follow are meant to address.

### 3. Replay protection and the storage cost of nonces
**What happened**
- [ethresear.ch: storage-optimized replay protection](https://ethresear.ch/t/storage-optimized-replay-protection-from-hash-bool-to-packed-flag-lanes-and-can-the-protocol-offer-better/26212), posted at 04:15 by dlebee. A privacy pool writes a nullifier on every withdrawal, Permit2's `nonceBitmap` and EIP-4337-style 2D nonces write replay flags, and machine settlement writes one flag per session. The most common `mapping(bytes32 => bool)` costs about 22,100 gas per flag (20,000 of it from the zero to non-zero write). ZK batching makes computation nearly free, but these flags cannot be compressed. The post compares packed flag lanes and other approaches and asks whether the protocol could provide a native primitive.

**Why it matters**
- Keyed nonces in native AA (such as EIP-8250) and privacy pool nullifiers are both directly exposed to storage pricing. Glamsterdam's EIP-8037 raises the cost of state creation, so these writes will get more expensive, and packed storage should come first when designing accounts and privacy protocols.
